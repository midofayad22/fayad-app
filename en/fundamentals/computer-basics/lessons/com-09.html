Input and Output Devices

Learning objectives

  • Classify devices as Input, Output or both.
  • Explain how physical signals become digital data and back, and the role of a Driver.
  • Compare common ports and calculate audio sizes and transfer times.

Prerequisites

  • The “How a Computer Works” (com-02) and motherboard (com-07) lessons.

Introduction: the bridge between person and machine

A computer deals only in numbers, while you deal in key presses, sounds, pictures and words. I/O Devices (input and output devices) translate between the two worlds. Input brings data from the outside world into the system, and Output sends processing results back to human senses or to another machine.

From the physical world to numbers

Most physical phenomena such as sound and light are continuous (analogue). A component called an ADC (analogue-to-digital converter) turns them into numbers by taking repeated measurements called Sampling. For sound, CD-quality audio is measured 44,100 times per second, each sample stored in 16 bits, for two channels (left and right). On the output side the reverse happens through a DAC, which turns numbers back into a voltage that moves the speaker.

A Driver is a small program that tells the operating system how to talk to one particular device; with it, devices from different makers look the same to software. An Interrupt is why response is quick: instead of asking every fraction of a second “was a key pressed?” (known as polling), the CPU waits to be alerted and attends only to what happened.

USB speeds vary with the version: USB 2.0 up to 480 megabits per second, USB 3.0 up to 5 gigabits, USB 3.2 Gen 2 up to 10 gigabits and USB4 up to 40 gigabits. Note that these figures are in bits (small b), not bytes, and a byte is 8 bits; so 480 Mbit/s is only 60 MB/s in theory.

Specifications that matter when choosing

  • Monitor: resolution (pixel count), refresh rate in hertz (how many times the image updates per second), response time and panel type.
  • Mouse: its DPI (dots per inch) sets how far the pointer moves for a given hand movement.
  • Printer: resolution in DPI and the print method, inkjet or laser.
  • Camera and microphone: resolution, sampling rate and sensor quality.

Real-world applications

  • Accessibility: screen readers, voice input and refreshable braille displays let people with disabilities use a computer.
  • Sensing: a phone contains motion, location and light sensors, all of which are input devices.
  • Industry and health: factory sensors and pulse monitors send continuous readings to monitoring systems.

Common mistakes

  • “The screen is part of the computer itself”: it is a peripheral output device with its own small controller and can be detached and swapped.
  • “A USB-C port means it is fast”: USB-C is only a connector shape and may carry slow USB 2.0 or fast USB4.
  • “A megabit is a megabyte”: they differ by a factor of 8, and mixing them makes estimates badly wrong.
  • “Every device works the moment it is plugged in with no driver”: many include generic drivers, but advanced features need a specific driver.
Exercise Calculation: how much space does one minute of CD-quality audio (44,100 samples per second, 16 bits, two channels) need?
Show solution

Bit rate = 44,100 × 16 × 2 = 1,411,200 bits per second. For a minute: 1,411,200 × 60 = 84,672,000 bits. Dividing by 8 gives 10,584,000 bytes, about 10.6 MB. That is before any compression, which is why MP3 files come out roughly ten times smaller.

Exercise Calculation: how long does moving 1 GB take over USB 2.0 (480 Mbit/s in theory)? And USB 3.0 (5 Gbit/s)? What should we expect in practice?
Show solution

1 GB = 8,000 megabits. Over USB 2.0: 8,000 ÷ 480 ≈ 16.7 s. Over USB 3.0: 8,000 ÷ 5,000 = 1.6 s. In practice times are longer: USB 2.0 typically drops to 30–40 MB/s, which takes roughly 25–35 s, and on USB 3.0 the speed of the attached stick or drive usually limits the transfer rather than the port.

Exercise Hands-on: list everything attached to or built into your device, classify each as input, output or both, and identify the available ports and their speeds.
Show solution

Example for a laptop: keyboard, touchpad, webcam and microphone (input); screen and speakers (output); touchscreen, Wi-Fi card and USB port (both). To learn a port's speed look at its symbol: SS means 5 Gbit/s or more, a lightning bolt means Thunderbolt, and with no symbol consult the device manual. In Device Manager (Windows) or System Report (macOS) you can see the connected USB devices and the driver version.

Summary

  • Input devices bring data in, output devices present results, and some devices do both.
  • Physical signals become numbers through an ADC and return through a DAC, while drivers and interrupts ensure understanding and speed.
  • A port's shape does not set its speed, and watch the difference between bits and bytes.

Hands-on practice

Try it yourself

Classify Mouse, Monitor, Keyboard, and Speaker as Input or Output.

Test yourself

  1. Which device is an Input Device?

    • Monitor
    • Speaker
    • Keyboard
    • Projector
    Answer and explanation

    Correct answer: Keyboard

  2. USB 2.0 is rated at 480 Mbit/s. What is that in MB/s in theory?

    • 480 MB/s
    • 3840 MB/s
    • 60 MB/s
    • 4.8 MB/s
    Answer and explanation

    Correct answer: 60 MB/s

    A byte is 8 bits, so 480 / 8 = 60 MB/s.

  3. What is the benefit of an interrupt compared with polling?

    • It makes the CPU ask the device every moment
    • It stops the device sending data
    • It increases memory size
    • The device alerts the CPU when something happens so the CPU need not keep asking
    Answer and explanation

    Correct answer: The device alerts the CPU when something happens so the CPU need not keep asking

    Waiting to be alerted saves CPU time and keeps the response quick.

  4. Which device counts as both input and output?

    • Keyboard
    • Network card
    • Monitor
    • Mouse
    Answer and explanation

    Correct answer: Network card

    A network card receives and sends data, as do touchscreens and USB sticks.

  5. What does an ADC do?

    • Turns numbers into sound
    • Turns analogue phenomena such as sound and light into numbers by repeated sampling
    • Prints pages
    • Charges the device
    Answer and explanation

    Correct answer: Turns analogue phenomena such as sound and light into numbers by repeated sampling

    This process is called sampling, and the DAC does the reverse on output.
