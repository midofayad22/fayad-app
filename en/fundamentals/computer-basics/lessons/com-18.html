History of Computers

Learning objectives

  • Order the main milestones of computing history with their correct approximate dates.
  • Explain the first four generations and what each technological leap changed.
  • Describe the Von Neumann architecture and Moore's law carefully and without exaggeration.

Prerequisites

  • The lessons “How a computer works”, “The CPU” and “Binary and data”.

Introduction: why read the history?

The history of computing is not a list of dates to memorise but a story of successive problems: how do we calculate faster, make the machine smaller, make it cheap enough for everyone? Each answer opened a new door. Knowing the reason behind each step helps you see why your device works the way it does, and that today's machines are the accumulation of ideas centuries old.

Generations and what they changed

Historians divide electronic computers into generations by the main technology used to build them. The boundaries are approximate, but the idea explains the overall direction: each generation was smaller, faster, cheaper and less power-hungry than the last.

GenerationTechnologyApprox. periodWhat changed
FirstVacuum tubes1940s - mid-1950sroom-sized machines that used much power and failed often
SecondTransistorsmid-1950s - mid-1960ssmaller and more reliable, and high-level languages appeared
ThirdIntegrated circuitsmid-1960s - early 1970scheaper and faster, and multitasking operating systems began
FourthMicroprocessors1971 to todaypersonal computers and then smartphones built around a chip-sized CPU

Some people speak of a “fifth generation” tied to artificial intelligence, but that is a loose label not everyone agrees on.

Moore's law

In 1965 Gordon Moore, later a co-founder of Intel, observed that the number of components that could economically fit on a chip was doubling roughly every year, and in 1975 he revised the forecast to about every two years. Moore's Law is therefore an empirical observation and an industry forecast, not a law of physics. It held for decades because the industry made it a target, but it has slowed in recent years as transistors approach physical limits and manufacturing costs rise. So the industry now turns to multi-core processors and specialised accelerators instead of relying on clock speed alone.

The arithmetic lands near the transistor counts of today's very large processors, which hold tens of billions, and shows how steady doubling accumulates. It is an illustrative estimate, not the exact figure of any one product.

The Von Neumann architecture

In a 1945 draft report John von Neumann described a design that is still the basis of most computers: a processing unit (arithmetic, logic and control), a single memory holding instructions and data together, and input and output devices. If the program is stored in memory it can be changed by loading another program without rewiring, which early ENIAC required. It has a cost known as the Von Neumann Bottleneck: the single path between processor and memory can become a choke point, which is why modern processors add a fast cache.

Real-world applications

  • Understanding vocabulary: the word “bug” comes from the era of electromechanical machines, and “computer” from the people who calculated by hand.
  • Buying decisions: knowing that performance does not come from clock speed alone but from cores and cache protects you from misleading ads.
  • Software design: the bottleneck explains why the way a program accesses memory affects its speed.
  • Historical perspective: you can see that each new technology (the network, then the phone) created industries nobody had imagined before.

Common mistakes

  • “ENIAC was the very first computer”: there were earlier machines such as Britain's Colossus in World War II; ENIAC stands out as a large general-purpose electronic computer.
  • “Moore's law is an unchanging law of nature”: it is an industry observation that has slowed, and it concerns transistor counts rather than device speed doubling every two years.
  • “Babbage built the first computer”: he designed the Analytical Engine in detail but it was never completed, so it is the first concept, not the first working machine.
  • “The internet and the web appeared together”: the network preceded the web by about two decades, ARPANET in 1969 and the web in the late 1980s and early 1990s.
Exercise Ordering: put these in time order: iPhone, transistor, Intel 4004, ENIAC, integrated circuit. Then name the generation each belongs to where possible.
Show solution

The order is ENIAC (1945-1946), the transistor (1947), the integrated circuit (1958-1959), the Intel 4004 (1971), then the iPhone (2007). ENIAC is first generation (vacuum tubes), the transistor underlies the second, the integrated circuit the third, and the 4004 begins the fourth. The iPhone is a fourth-generation product built on microprocessors, not a new generation.

Exercise Calculation: if transistor counts double every two years, by what factor do they grow in ten years? And if doubling slows to every three years?
Show solution

Ten years with doubling every two years is five doublings: 25 = 32 times. With doubling every three years: 10 ÷ 3 ≈ 3.33 doublings, so 23.33 is about 10 times. A slowdown that looks small in the exponent makes a big difference after only a few years, which is why it matters to the industry.

Summary

  • Computing evolved from mechanical machines to theoretical designs (Babbage and Turing) and then to electronic computers.
  • The transistor, integrated circuit and microprocessor were the leaps that made computers small and cheap.
  • Moore's law is an observation about transistor counts, not a natural law, and it has slowed.
  • The Von Neumann architecture keeps instructions and data in one memory and is still the foundation.

  1. Which technology is associated with the first generation of computers?

    • Transistors
    • Vacuum Tubes
    • Microprocessors
    • Cloud Computing
    Answer and explanation

    Correct answer: Vacuum Tubes

  2. Which approximate chronological order is correct?

    • Transistor, then ENIAC, then Pascaline
    • Pascaline, then ENIAC, then the transistor
    • ENIAC, then Pascaline, then the transistor
    • Pascaline, then the transistor, then ENIAC
    Answer and explanation

    Correct answer: Pascaline, then ENIAC, then the transistor

    The Pascaline is from 1642, ENIAC about 1946 and the transistor 1947.

  3. Which invention put a whole CPU on one chip in 1971?

    • The integrated circuit
    • The Intel 4004 microprocessor
    • The vacuum tube
    • The transistor
    Answer and explanation

    Correct answer: The Intel 4004 microprocessor

    The integrated circuit combined transistors, while the 4004 put the whole processor on a chip, starting the fourth generation.

  4. What replaced the vacuum tube, making second generation computers smaller and more reliable?

    • The microprocessor
    • RAM
    • The transistor
    • Jacquard punched cards
    Answer and explanation

    Correct answer: The transistor

    The transistor is a small electronic switch with less heat and fewer failures than a tube.

  5. Which statement about Moore's law is the most accurate?

    • A law of physics that never fails
    • An observation that transistors per chip roughly doubled every couple of years
    • It says internet speed doubles every year
    • It says prices rise every year
    Answer and explanation

    Correct answer: An observation that transistors per chip roughly doubled every couple of years

    It is a historical observation, not a natural law, and it has slowed near physical limits.
