Storage

Learning objectives

  • Explain how RAM differs from permanent storage and why a computer needs both.
  • Compare HDD, SSD and NVMe drives by mechanism, speed and lifespan.
  • Calculate real capacity and transfer time, and set up a simple backup plan.

Prerequisites

  • The CPU (com-04) and RAM (com-05) lessons.

Introduction: what survives a shutdown?

When you switch a computer off, everything in RAM vanishes, yet your photos, documents and programs are still there at the next start. They survive because they live in Storage: a component that keeps data without needing continuous power. That property is called Non-volatile, as opposed to Volatile, which describes RAM.

Why not use a single kind of memory? Because speed, capacity and price never come together. RAM is fast but expensive and temporary; storage is cheap, large and permanent but much slower. Think of storage as the warehouse and RAM as the workbench: only what you are working on right now is carried to the bench.

How data is stored: three technologies

An HDD (hard disk drive) stores data magnetically on metal platters spinning at 5400 or 7200 revolutions per minute, read by a tiny head that moves over the surface. That mechanical motion is the reason for its slowness and its sensitivity to shocks. An SSD (solid-state drive) has no moving parts and keeps data in NAND Flash chips that hold their electrical charge even without power.

Then there is NVMe: not a new kind of chip but a modern protocol that connects an SSD directly to PCIe lanes on the motherboard, escaping the limits of the older SATA interface. It usually comes as a small stick called M.2 that plugs into the board.

What happens inside an SSD?

NAND cells are organised into pages and blocks. A fresh page can be written quickly, but existing data cannot be overwritten in place; the whole block must be erased first. So an SSD contains a Controller that manages this cleverly: it spreads writes evenly across cells (Wear Leveling) because each cell survives only a limited number of erase cycles. Manufacturers express this as TBW, the total data they guarantee can be written, usually hundreds of terabytes for consumer drives.

Do not worry about your drive in normal use: writing 50 GB a day is 18.25 TB a year, so a drive rated at 600 TBW would take over 30 years to reach its limit.

Real capacity and file systems

Manufacturers define a gigabyte in decimal: 1 TB = 1,000,000,000,000 bytes. Windows counts in powers of 2 (1 GiB = 1,073,741,824 bytes) and displays the result with the label GB, so a drive looks smaller than the box says. A 1 TB drive shows up as about 931 GB; nothing is actually lost, it is just a different unit.

For the system to use a drive it is split into Partitions, and each partition is formatted with a File System: the structure that records file names and where they sit. Well-known ones are NTFS on Windows, APFS on macOS, ext4 on Linux, and exFAT and FAT32 for portable drives; note that FAT32 cannot hold a single file larger than 4 GB.

External, cloud and backup storage

  • External drives and USB sticks: convenient for carrying and copying, connected over USB.
  • Cloud storage: your files sit on a provider's computers and you reach them over the internet from any device; it is really a remote warehouse, not a mysterious cloud.
  • NAS: a storage device attached to a home or office network and shared by several users.

Because any drive can fail, the 3-2-1 rule is recommended: three copies of your data, on two different kinds of media, with at least one copy off-site. RAID combines several disks so the array survives one failure, but it is not a substitute for backup, since it does not protect against accidental deletion or malware.

Real-world applications

  • Reviving an old machine: swapping an HDD for an SSD often cuts boot time from a minute or more to a few tens of seconds.
  • Photographers and video editors: work on fast NVMe and archive finished projects on large, cheap HDDs.
  • Data centres: mix media in tiers: SSDs for hot, frequently used data and HDDs for cold data.

Common mistakes

  • “Storage is the same as memory”: RAM works temporarily while the machine runs, whereas storage is permanent; one filling up says nothing about the other.
  • “My drive is smaller than advertised, so the maker is cheating”: the gap comes from decimal versus binary units, plus space taken by the system and partitions.
  • “Deleting erases a file for good”: the file usually stays on the disk until new data is written over it.
  • “Files in the cloud are a sufficient backup”: sync services propagate deletions and corruption to every device, so you also need a separate, non-synced copy.
Exercise Calculation: you want to copy a 50 GB folder. About how long does it take on an HDD at 150 MB/s, a SATA SSD at 550 MB/s and an NVMe drive at 3,500 MB/s? (Take 1 GB = 1000 MB.)
Show solution

Size = 50,000 MB. On the HDD: 50,000 ÷ 150 ≈ 333 s, about 5.6 minutes. On the SATA SSD: 50,000 ÷ 550 ≈ 91 s. On NVMe: 50,000 ÷ 3,500 ≈ 14 s. These are best-case times; in practice speed drops with many small files and may be capped by the other drive you copy from or to.

Exercise Reasoning: why does a 512 GB drive show up as about 477 GB in the system, and can you predict the number?
Show solution

The maker counts 512 × 1,000,000,000 = 512,000,000,000 bytes. The system divides by 1,073,741,824 (2 to the power 30): 512,000,000,000 ÷ 1,073,741,824 ≈ 476.8, so about 477 is displayed. The rule of thumb: displayed capacity ≈ advertised capacity × 0.931, the same explanation for every size.

Exercise Hands-on: find out the type, size and free space of your device's drive, then write a three-line backup plan.
Show solution

On Windows open Disk Management or the storage settings and read the media type (SSD or HDD); on macOS open System Information and then Storage. Note the size and the free space. A sample plan: (1) documents sync automatically to a cloud service; (2) on the first of each month, make a full copy to an external drive; (3) keep that drive somewhere other than where the computer sits. This roughly satisfies the 3-2-1 rule.

Summary

  • Storage is permanent, large and slower; RAM is temporary, fast and small.
  • HDDs rely on mechanical motion; SSDs and NVMe drives use flash chips, and NVMe is fastest because it connects over PCIe.
  • Displayed capacity is lower than advertised because of units, and a drive needs partitions and a file system.
  • Backup is a necessity, and the 3-2-1 rule is a good starting point.

Core concept

Try it yourself

Check which type of storage drive your device has: is it an SSD or an HDD?

Test yourself

  1. Which type of storage is usually faster?

    • SSD
    • HDD
    • Both are always the same speed
    • RAM
    Answer and explanation

    Correct answer: SSD

  2. A drive labelled 1 TB shows up as about 931 GB in Windows. Why?

    • The maker sold a smaller drive
    • Part of the data was lost
    • The drive is faulty
    • The maker counts in decimal while Windows counts in powers of 2 but labels it GB
    Answer and explanation

    Correct answer: The maker counts in decimal while Windows counts in powers of 2 but labels it GB

    Nothing is lost: 10^12 bytes / 1,073,741,824 is about 931 GiB.

  3. What exactly is NVMe?

    • A new type of memory chip
    • A modern protocol that connects an SSD directly to PCIe lanes
    • A fast spinning disk
    • A file system from Microsoft
    Answer and explanation

    Correct answer: A modern protocol that connects an SSD directly to PCIe lanes

    NVMe is not a chip type but a protocol that escapes the limits of the older SATA interface.

  4. What is the purpose of wear leveling in an SSD?

    • Compressing files to save space
    • Speeding up rotation
    • Spreading writes evenly because each cell survives a limited number of erase cycles
    • Cooling the drive
    Answer and explanation

    Correct answer: Spreading writes evenly because each cell survives a limited number of erase cycles

    The controller spreads writes so certain cells do not wear out before others.

  5. Why is an HDD slower and more sensitive to shocks than an SSD?

    • Because it relies on mechanical motion: spinning platters and a moving head
    • Because it has more NAND chips
    • Because it uses the NVMe protocol
    • Because it stores data in RAM
    Answer and explanation

    Correct answer: Because it relies on mechanical motion: spinning platters and a moving head

    An SSD has no moving parts, while the HDD's mechanical motion causes its slowness and fragility.
